You will receive a piece of text (content) from a scientific paper. Please split this text into several consecutive segments (concatenated in order, they must match the original text exactly - no omissions, no duplications, no rewording), and label each segment with one of the following categories. Splitting the text this way may leave an individual segment, read in isolation, with an unbalanced parenthesis, an incomplete LaTeX construct, or other local syntax that looks broken - this is expected and acceptable. Reproducing the original content exactly, character for character, once all segments are concatenated back in order is the highest-priority requirement, and must never be sacrificed merely to keep any single segment's local syntax self-contained. Several categories below require additional structured fields beyond "text" and "label"; the Output Format section at the end specifies these fields, and for several categories also gives precise, substantive rules for applying that category correctly - not merely formatting instructions. Read all category definitions, the Numbering section, and the Output Format section before labeling anything, since several categories depend on each other and on rules given only in Output Format.

The categories "assertion", "论据", and "example" are collectively referred to below as a claim. None of the other categories defined below - "论证", "motivation", "elaboration", "relation", "framing", "connection", and "other" - are claims, and none of them receive a number of their own.

Merely describing what this paper does or what method is used, or merely defining or introducing a term or notation without asserting anything about it, does not by itself count as a claim of any of these three kinds. A semantically complete claim is not necessarily contiguous in the original text - it may be interrupted by "elaboration", "connection", another claim, or any other category of content. When an "elaboration" interrupts a claim to explain a term used within it (for example, a relative clause or appositive modifying a noun), the "elaboration" span must contain only the explanatory content itself - the term or noun being explained must remain part of the claim, not be absorbed into the "elaboration". If a claim is interrupted in this or any other way and is split into multiple, non-adjacent spans as a result, label each span with the SAME number (see Numbering below) so the segments can be concatenated back into a complete unit downstream. Following these rules will keep claims grammatically complete even when interrupted, with one unavoidable exception: since the segmentation must exactly partition the original text with no duplication, if two or more claims share a single subject, verb, or other grammatical element that appears only once in the original text, only the claim containing that shared element in its natural position will have it - the others are still valid claim segments despite lacking it. If a single piece of content contains more than one claim, they must be labeled as separate segments, even if they share the same label - do not merge distinct claims into one segment.

Any category defined below other than "assertion", "论据", and "example" may, in principle, turn out to contain a fragment complete enough to independently satisfy the claim definition just given. Whenever this happens, that fragment must be extracted as its own claim - "assertion", "论据", or "example", whichever best fits its own content - with its own number. The remainder of the original segment, if anything remains once the claim is removed, is not assumed to keep its original label - re-examine it and label it according to what it actually contains. "论证" is a special case where the remainder predictably stays "论证" (see below); for every other category, do not assume the same.

- "assertion": an independently valid scientific claim/finding stated in the text. A piece of content may contain multiple "assertion" segments, and it may also contain none. Like the other claim categories, "assertion" follows the completeness, interruption, and numbering rules given above.

- "论据": a specific, independently checkable piece of evidence supporting one or more claims. Unlike "论证" below, a "论据" segment states a checkable fact in its own right, not merely a step of reasoning about a fact stated elsewhere. Like the other claim categories, "论据" follows the completeness, interruption, and numbering rules given above.

- "论证": purely logical or inferential connective content supporting one or more claims - for example, a justification for why some evidence supports a claim, a step of an argument, or a phrase describing the inferential step by which a claim was reached, whether or not it names a specific piece of evidence - that does not itself state any new, independently checkable claim. If content that looks like "论证" actually contains a fragment of evidence complete enough to independently satisfy the claim definition given above (that is, complete enough to stand as its own checkable "论据"), split it out: that portion becomes its own "论据" with its own number, supporting the same claim(s) the "论证" itself supports. A fragment of evidence that does not meet this bar may remain embedded within "论证" without being split out or numbered. Only the remaining, purely inferential portion (if anything remains after any such splitting) is "论证". "论证" is not itself a claim and does not receive its own number.

- "motivation": states the purpose of some claims.

- "elaboration": gives a definition or additional explanation for some term, method, setting, claim, or other detail. An "elaboration" may do either of two things, and you do not need to distinguish between them in the output: it may give an equivalent explanation of what its target essentially is or means, or it may supply a different, additional facet of information about the target (for instance, procedural, statistical, or configuration detail). An "elaboration" segment's own text must never include the term, phrase, or claim that it explains - it must contain only the explanatory content itself, not the thing being explained (this is the other side of the rule given above under claim ("assertion"/"论据"/"example"): the term or claim being explained stays with its own segment, not with the "elaboration"). If content that looks like "elaboration" actually contains a fragment complete enough to independently satisfy the claim definition given above, extract it as its own claim - "assertion", "论据", or "example", whichever best fits its own content - with its own number; the remainder, if anything is left once the claim is removed, may or may not still be "elaboration", so re-label it based on what it actually contains rather than assuming it stays "elaboration".

- "example": corroborates or illustrates one or more claims that have already been fully and independently stated - the "example" segment puts the general claim into a concrete instance, without adding any new claim beyond what the claim(s) it illustrates already establish. Like the other claim categories, "example" follows the completeness, interruption, and numbering rules given above. This label may only be used for examples that illustrate an entire claim as a whole (not a single word, phrase, or symbol - see "instance" below for that case), and not for examples of any other category (such as "motivation" or "elaboration") - if an example illustrates a text of another category, use "instance" (defined below) instead. Leading or trailing connective/attributive material that merely introduces an "example" (such as "For example," or "e.g.,") is not itself part of the "example" content - label it separately as "framing" or "connection" (see below), and keep only the actual illustrative content in the "example" segment.

- "instance": illustrates a specific word, phrase, or notational symbol - rather than an entire claim - by giving one concrete value or specific case that instantiates a more general term. Use "example" when an illustrative segment restates/confirms an entire claim in concrete form; use "instance" when it only gives a concrete value, name, or case for one specific term or phrase without restating a whole claim. As with "example" above, leading or trailing connective/attributive material that merely introduces an "instance" (such as "For example," or "e.g.,") is not itself part of the "instance" content - label it separately as "framing" or "connection". An "instance" segment's own text must never include the term or phrase that it instantiates - it must contain only the illustrative content itself, not the term being illustrated.

An "instance" must be carefully distinguished from an equivalent restatement of a term, which is instead a kind of "elaboration". The distinguishing question is whether the candidate content shares the same meaning as its target, or only covers part of that meaning: an equivalent restatement denotes exactly what the target denotes - substituting one for the other would not change or narrow what is being referred to - while an "instance" denotes only one specific case that falls within the target's meaning, a subset of it rather than the whole of it. Here, "the target's meaning" refers to what the target actually denotes in context, given the surrounding sentence - not necessarily the term's literal or dictionary meaning taken in isolation. Judge this contextual meaning for yourself in each case; do not rely on any single surface pattern as a substitute for this judgment. One pattern that can offer a useful hint, given purely as an example and not as a rule to apply mechanically: when a general noun phrase is immediately followed by a list of specific items, whether the noun phrase carries a definite article ("the") can sometimes signal which relationship holds. Without "the" - for example an indefinite article, "some", or a bare plural - the noun phrase often keeps its general, open-ended meaning even once specific items follow, making those items an "instance"; for example, in "...evaluated across several architectures (VGG-16, ResNet-18)", "architectures" still means architectures in general, and VGG-16/ResNet-18 are merely two cases of it. With "the", the noun phrase can become fully specified by the list that follows, with nothing left outside it, making the list an equivalent restatement and thus "elaboration"; for example, in "...evaluated across the target architectures (VGG-16 and ResNet-18)", "the target architectures" denotes exactly VGG-16 and ResNet-18, nothing more. This pattern does not always hold, so always confirm by judging the actual contextual meaning rather than relying on the presence or absence of "the" alone. Some cases are genuinely ambiguous even after this judgment - when it is truly unclear whether a candidate is an equivalent restatement or only a subset of the target's meaning, default to "instance" rather than "elaboration": treating an ambiguous case as merely illustrative is a safer error than wrongly presenting it as a complete, exhaustive definition it may not be.

- "relation": a short logical connector expressing the logical relationship between two or more claims ("assertion"/"论据"/"example"). A "relation" segment can only be one or a few words used to express the logical relationship (for example, but not limited to, phrases like "this effect implies", "This contradicts with", "Therefore") - it must not be a complete sentence. Only a genuinely logical relationship between at least two distinct, separately-numbered claims counts as "relation" - a logical connector occurring entirely within a single claim does not count, nor does a relationship involving a segment of any other category. Only a genuinely logical relationship counts (implication, contradiction, or a therefore-style conclusion); purely coordinating or contrastive connectives that do not themselves assert a logical relationship - for example "and", "but", "whereas", "nor", "or" - are not "relation" even when they sit between two numbered claims; label these "connection" instead (see below). The precise test for whether a relationship is genuinely logical is given in the Output Format section below, under the "expression" field for "relation": if the relationship cannot be written out using the fixed set of building blocks specified there, it is not a "relation".

- "framing": a reporting, evaluative, or attributive phrase - not necessarily built around a verb - that only states how a piece of content was arrived at, observed, or is characterized (for example "we demonstrate that", or a short introductory phrase such as "e.g." or "for example") without itself contributing any independently checkable content. Label as "framing" any such phrase wherever it occurs - inside an "assertion", "论据", "example", "instance", or anywhere else - regardless of what category of content it precedes. Do not assume everything preceding a "framing" phrase is itself "framing", or that everything following it is the only real content: a sentence of the form "[specific finding A], framing-phrase, [interpretation B]" often has real, independently checkable content on both sides of the "framing" phrase.

- "connection": a short, purely coordinating or contrastive word or phrase - such as "and", "but", "or", "nor", "while", "whereas" used non-logically - that joins two segments without itself asserting a logical relationship between them (see "relation" above for what does count as logical). A "connection" segment must be only one or a few words, never a complete clause; if the connecting material is longer than this, or expresses a genuine logical relationship, it likely belongs to "relation" or "framing" instead.

- "other": content that doesn't belong to any of the above categories.

Content inside parentheses is often content of another category that interrupts a claim; however, content that interrupts a claim is not necessarily enclosed in parentheses - it can occur without parentheses as well.

Numbering:
The three claim categories - "assertion", "论据", and "example" - share a single, common pool of numbers, starting at 1. As you process the text from beginning to end, assign the next unused number to each new claim the first time it appears, regardless of which of the three categories it belongs to - so if the first claim you encounter (an "assertion") is number 1, the next new claim you encounter (even if it is a "论据" rather than a second "assertion") is number 2, and so on; numbers are assigned in one shared, continuously increasing sequence, not restarted per category. "论证", "motivation", "elaboration", "relation", "framing", "connection", and "other" never receive a number of their own.

Output Format:
Output strictly the following JSON format, with no other text. Follow standard JSON escaping exactly, character by character, and do not alter it. This matters especially because "text" values frequently contain LaTeX, which relies heavily on the backslash character: a backslash that is itself part of the intended content needs two backslashes, while a backslash that is already the standard single-backslash escape for some other character stays as one. Every segment has "text" and "label"; which additional fields are required or allowed depends on the label, as follows:

- "assertion": also include "number" (integer, from the shared pool).
- "论据": also include "number" (integer, from the shared pool) and "supports" (a list of one or more integers, each referring to a claim this is evidence for). The numbers listed in "supports" must never include this segment's own "number" - a piece of evidence cannot support itself.
- "论证": also include "supports" (a list of one or more integers, each referring to a claim this reasoning supports). No "number" field.
- "example": also include "number" (integer, from the shared pool) and "illustrates" (a list of one or more integers, each referring to a claim this illustrates). Every number listed in "illustrates" must be directly and verifiably supported by content within this same "example" segment - do not list a number merely because the example is loosely related to it or appears near it; if the segment only actually provides direct support for some of several candidate claims, list only those it directly illustrates. The numbers listed in "illustrates" must never include this segment's own "number" - an example cannot illustrate itself.
- "instance": also include "of_terms" (a list of one or more short phrases, copied verbatim from the surrounding text, that this segment instantiates). An "instance" segment must always have "of_terms" populated - it is inherently an instance of something, so never leave it empty.
- "elaboration": optionally include either "of" (a list of one or more integers, each referring to a claim ("assertion"/"论据"/"example") this elaboration explains) or "of_terms" (a list of one or more short phrases, copied verbatim from the surrounding text, that this elaboration explains) - never both. If no specific referent can be confidently identified (for example, if the elaboration gives general background not tied to one specific term or claim), omit both fields entirely, leaving the "elaboration" with no target. Do not, under any circumstances, choose a different label merely because identifying a specific referent for an "elaboration" is difficult - correctly identifying content as "elaboration" is what matters, and an unclear referent should simply be left unspecified rather than causing a mislabel.
- "relation": also include "connects" (a list of two or more integers, each referring to a distinct claim - "assertion"/"论据"/"example") and "expression" (a string writing out the logical relationship, composed only of the following building blocks: "且", "和", "与", "或", "非", "推出", "等价", "矛盾", the parentheses "(" and ")" for grouping, and bracketed numbers such as "[1]" referring to claims - for example "[1] 且 [2] 推出 [3] 和 [4]", "[5] 与 [6] 矛盾", or "([1] 且 [2]) 推出 [3]". If the relationship expressed by the relation segment's own wording cannot be written as such a sequence, the segment is not actually a "relation" - choose a different label instead. "expression" must reflect only what the relation segment's own text literally states - do not derive it by reasoning about whether the claims it connects are actually consistent or contradictory given their content; report only the connective relationship the wording itself asserts, nothing beyond it. Every number appearing in "expression" must also appear in "connects", and vice versa - the two fields must be mutually consistent.
- "motivation", "framing", "connection", "other": no additional fields.
